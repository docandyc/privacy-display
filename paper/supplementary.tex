\section{Scope}

This supplement preserves the object-detection and tracking diagnostics that were removed from the main manuscript. These experiments probe whether temporal degradation affects non-text visual tasks, but they do not carry the paper's primary conventional-OCR claim. The real-capture detection/tracking runs use one camera position and limited datasets; the simulated tracking metrics are approximate and not official TrackEval/ByteTrack results.

\section{Real-Capture Object Detection and Tracking}
\label{sec:supp_real_detection}

To probe whether image degradation extends beyond text, we ran an exploratory detection/tracking stress test within the same camera link. Content was displayed on the 240\,Hz screen, captured by the S600 at 1.5\,m / $0^{\circ}$, and processed by four detectors~\cite{b_yolo26,b_rtdetr2024,b_fasterrcnn2015,b_retinanet2017}. The protected condition uses the $n=4$ mask+noise profile without stripe/glyph overlays or inversion. The \texttt{real\_clean} reference is an unprotected 31.25-ms capture, \texttt{real\_short} is one protected 3.91-ms capture selected from a three-frame burst, and \texttt{real\_video} is the mean of 150 protected 3.91-ms frames acquired at nominally 60\,fps. Consequently, protection and exposure are not isolated in the clean-versus-short comparison. Tracking uses 450~frames from MOT17-09-FRCNN via still-display-then-capture rather than continuous video synchronization (Table~\ref{tab:supp_real_mot}); the associator is a greedy-fallback implementation inspired by ByteTrack~\cite{b_bytetrack2022}, not official ByteTrack.

Across the four detectors, mAP50 is 39.2--50.2\% for \texttt{real\_clean}, 1.6--5.8\% for \texttt{real\_short}, and 3.0--7.3\% for \texttt{real\_video} (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_real_det_drop}). These differences are descriptive diagnostics, not protection effects, because the unprotected and protected conditions use different exposures and no luminance-matched static control was captured. In still-frame tracking, HOTA is 14.2--15.8\% for \texttt{real\_clean} and 5.1--8.7\% for \texttt{real\_short}; IDF1 is 9.6--12.1\% and 4.4--6.5\%, respectively. The clean baseline is itself depressed by the still-frame camera link, and the procedure cannot substitute for continuous-video tracking.

\section{Software Detection and Tracking Simulation}
\label{sec:supp_sim_detection}

Supplementary simulations were run on COCO val2017~\cite{b_coco2014} and MOT17~\cite{b_mot16}. The four detectors are YOLO26x~\cite{b_yolo26}, RT-DETR-x~\cite{b_rtdetr2024}, Faster R-CNN~\cite{b_fasterrcnn2015}, and RetinaNet~\cite{b_retinanet2017}. The COCO simulation used only 8~images, positioned as implementation pipeline diagnostics (Table~\ref{tab:supp_coco_sim}). The MOT simulation covers 7~sequences totaling 5,316~frames, using a ByteTrack-inspired~\cite{b_bytetrack2022} greedy association fallback implementation (Table~\ref{tab:supp_mot_sim}). Because TrackEval failed to run, MOTA/MOTP/IDF1 in the table come from an in-project approximate evaluation backend and are not equivalent to official ByteTrack/TrackEval results.

On these 8~images, single-subframe mAP50 across four detectors is 0--6.0\%. This sample size is sufficient only to check trends, not to estimate COCO population performance. In the 5,316-frame approximate tracking results, single-subframe IDF1 is 0.1--5.4\%, though MOTA exhibits negative values and nonmonotonic behavior. Negative MOTA means the approximate backend's false-positive, false-negative, and identity-error penalties exceed the number of ground-truth objects; it is not a negative detection probability. Differences among YOLO26x, RT-DETR-x, Faster R-CNN, and RetinaNet may reflect architecture, confidence thresholds, proposal density, and interaction with the greedy associator, but this experiment does not isolate those factors. We therefore do not assign a causal mechanism to the cross-detector spread.
